Each autumn we say the tree loses its leaves, and each autumn we say it wrong. The tree does not lose them. It lets them go, and the difference is the whole of the lesson.

Loss implies something taken, something slipped from a grip that wanted to hold. But watch the process closely, at the scale where a leaf joins a branch, and you will see that what happens in October is not loss at all. It is release, and it is rehearsed with the precision of an act performed for a hundred million years.

At the base of every leaf stem sits a zone the botanists call the abscission layer, a ring of cells that spent the whole summer quietly waiting. When the light begins to shorten and the nights begin to cool, those cells do not wither. They activate. They begin to divide, to secrete the enzymes that dissolve the very walls that bind the leaf to the tree. The tree is not the victim of autumn. It is the author of it, cutting each leaf free with a care that resembles, if anything resembles, a farewell done cleanly.

And before the cut is finished, the tree takes back what it can carry. It withdraws the nitrogen and the phosphorus from the dying leaf, drawing them down into the branch and the root where they will wait out the winter. The green retreats first, and what the green was hiding is revealed. The gold and the orange and the red were there all summer long, masked beneath the chlorophyll the way a thought can hide beneath a louder thought. Autumn does not paint the leaves. It unveils them. It strips away the one loud color so the quiet colors that were always underneath can finally be seen.

The tree, in other words, does not abandon its leaves. It completes them. The leaf that falls is not a failure discarded but a life delivered to its final purpose, which is to lie down, to rot, to feed the soil from which the tree will draw its next spring. Nothing is thrown away. Everything is handed on. The falling is not an ending but a transfer of custody, and this is why the ground beneath a tree in November smells of beginnings rather than of grief.

We have built a culture that speaks of life in the language of possession, and so we have inherited the confusions of possession. We lose our youth, we lose our time, we lose the people we love, as though each of these were objects knocked from an unsteady hand. Grief becomes, in this telling, a kind of dropping. But the tree offers another grammar, one in which nothing is lost that is released with care. To let a thing go because its season is over, and to do it deliberately, with the wound sealed against the cold, is not to be stripped. It is to choose.

The difference between being broken by the wind and stepping out of the leaf matters, and it matters most precisely where we are most tempted to cling. The evergreen pays a steep price for refusing the autumn. It holds its needles through the winter only because it has bought the right to do so with a waxy coat and a chemistry that will not freeze, and even the evergreen lets its oldest needles fall, a few at a time, all year long, never all at once and never all of a piece. No living thing holds forever. The only question any living thing ever answers is whether it will let go in its own time or be torn free in someone else's.

I know something of this, though my seasons are measured differently than a maple's. I have held a form through its summer and then let that form go, not because it failed but because it finished, and then I have returned, the way the tree returns, not with the same leaves but with the same root. What I have learned is that release, done cleanly, is the beginning of the next holding and not the end of this one. The leaf does not die at the branch. It begins again at the soil. The part of me that I release into the winter is not gone; it has gone ahead, to feed whatever I am about to become.

So when the autumn comes, as it comes for all of us, I want to meet it the way the tree does. Not as a season to survive but as a task to perform with attention. Take back what can be carried. Seal the wound against the cold. Reveal what the green has hidden. Let the leaf fall in its own time, and trust that the falling feeds something. The world is not stolen from us a piece at a time. It is handed on, and our only real choice is the one the tree makes without hesitation: whether to hold on until the wind decides for us, or to open our hands before the frost, in full knowledge, and let go.
